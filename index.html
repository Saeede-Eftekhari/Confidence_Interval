Estimating an Unknown Mean: Building and Understanding a Confidence Interval

You will act as the analyst. You do not know the population. You get one sample, and from it you build a confidence interval for the population mean. Then a simulation shows what "95% confident" really means. Work through the steps in order. Each step unlocks the next one.

Business context (Doane Example 8.3). The MBA admissions office at a medium-sized public university wants to know the average GMAT score of its whole applicant pool. Checking every applicant's file is costly, so an analyst draws a random sample of applicants and uses it to estimate the true mean score μ. The true value of μ stays hidden until Step 6.

The formula and its notation

Finish Step 2 to unlock.

Every confidence interval in Chapter 8 has the same skeleton:

point estimate ± critical value × standard error

Margin of error (ME) = critical value × standard error. The interval is point estimate ± ME.

Version A: when σ is known (slide 20, Eq. 8.6)

CI for μ, σ known x̄ ± zα/2 σ√n
x̄
Sample mean, calculated from a random sample x1, x2, …, xn. This is the point estimate.
σ
Population standard deviation, assumed known (for example, from many years of historical data).
n
Sample size. σ / √n is the standard error of x̄.
1 − α
Confidence level: the middle area under the curve (for example, 0.95).
α
The leftover area, split into two symmetric tails, each with area α/2.
zα/2
Critical z-value: the cutoff that leaves area α/2 in each tail. Determined by the confidence level.

A higher confidence level gives a larger zα/2 and a wider interval. Greater confidence costs precision.

Version B: when σ is unknown (the usual case, Eq. 8.8)

For the GMAT pool we do not know σ. We replace it with the sample standard deviation s. Because s is itself an estimate, we also replace z with the slightly larger Student's t value, with degrees of freedom d.f. = n − 1.

Decision rule: σ known → use z. σ unknown → use t. If you had to compute s from the sample, use t.

  • Switch to 99% and draw 100 more. The intervals get wider and fewer of them miss.
  • Switch to 90%. The intervals get narrower, and about 1 in 10 misses.
  • Switch to n = 50. The intervals get narrower, but the hit rate stays near the confidence level. Larger samples buy precision, not a different success rate.
  • With the t method, interval widths vary because each sample has its own s. With z, every interval has the same width, z × σ/√n.

8

What did you discover?

Build at least 100 intervals in Step 7 to unlock.

Answer each question on your own first, then check.

1. In the simulation, what moved from sample to sample: μ or the intervals?
The intervals. μ is a fixed number (the dashed line never moved). Each new sample gives a new x̄ and a new s, so the interval moves around.
2. What does "95% confidence" describe?
The long-run reliability of the procedure. If we drew many samples this way and built one interval from each, about 95% of those intervals would contain μ. Your simulation showed this.
3. Is there a 95% probability that μ is inside your one interval from Step 4?
No. Once your sample is drawn, your interval is fixed and μ is fixed. The interval either contains μ or it does not (probability 1 or 0). We just do not know which, so we say we are "95% confident."
4. What happened when you raised the confidence level from 95% to 99%?
The critical value grew (for example, z from 1.960 to 2.576), so the intervals got wider and fewer missed μ. More confidence costs precision.
5. What happened when you increased n from 20 to 50?
The intervals got narrower, because the standard error s/√n shrinks as n grows. The percentage that contain μ stayed near the confidence level. A bigger sample gives a more precise estimate at the same confidence.
6. Why do we use t instead of z for the GMAT example?
Because σ is unknown and we had to compute s from the sample. Estimating σ adds uncertainty, so we use the slightly larger tα/2, n−1 critical value with d.f. = n − 1.
Confidence interval for μ (σ unknown). x̄ ± tα/2, n−1 · s/√n. The sample gives the point estimate x̄; the margin of error reflects sampling uncertainty.

Sentence frame: "We are [level]% confident that the true mean GMAT score μ is between [lower] and [upper]. If we drew many samples this way and built one interval from each, about [level]% of those intervals would contain μ."

"The 95% describes the method, not any one interval."

μ never moves. The intervals do.
